Stories of the Sky

Kathā · கதை · कथा — the Vedic and Purāṇic tales behind the nine grahas and the nakṣatras, and what each one remembers about the real sky.

scroll or press → to begin
Before the stories

Three layers of telling

Vedic

In the Ṛgveda, Saṁhitās and Brāhmaṇas, Sūrya, Soma and Bṛhaspati are gods of the hymns; Svarbhānu is the darkness that seizes the Sun; the nakṣatras are listed with their deities. There is no “nine planets” yet — these are powers of the sacrifice and the sky.

Purāṇic & epic

The Purāṇas, Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa give the grahas families, marriages, quarrels and curses — Sūrya's wives, Soma's twenty-seven brides, the churning of the ocean. Versions differ between texts, and the page says which one it follows.

Living tradition

Temple legends, festivals and family practice — especially in Tamil Nadu — carry the stories on: Navagraha shrines around Kumbakonam, Thiruvathirai for Naṭarāja, Thai Pūsam for Murugan. Where a tale is a popular retelling rather than a text, it is marked so.

Read each story twice — once as a story, and once as a memory of the sky. Many of them are astronomy told in the language of gods.

☉
Graha one · Sūrya
☉

The wife who could not bear his light

Sūrya, Saṁjñā and Chāyā

Sūrya, son of Kaśyapa and Aditi, married Saṁjñā, daughter of the divine craftsman Viśvakarmā. She bore him Manu, Yama and Yamunā — but she could not endure his blazing heat. Leaving her own shadow, Chāyā, in her place, she slipped away to the forest in the form of a mare.

Chāyā bore Sūrya three more children, among them Śani. When she favoured her own sons over Saṁjñā's, Yama protested — and the deception came to light. Sūrya went in search of his wife, found her as a mare and became a horse himself; from that union the twin physicians, the Aśvins, were born.

To bring her home, Viśvakarmā set Sūrya on his lathe and trimmed away part of his brilliance. From the shavings he forged the weapons of the gods — among them Viṣṇu's discus, the Sudarśana.

PurāṇicMārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa (the Saṁjñā episode); Viṣṇu Purāṇa 3.2. Vedic Sūrya's chariot of seven bay mares, Ṛgveda 1.50.8; the Gāyatrī to Savitṛ, Ṛgveda 3.62.10.

Jyotiṣa echo

The Sun is father and ātman — and his household is crowded with the Jyotiṣa cast: Śani is his son, which is why the classical friendship tables make Sun and Saturn enemies; Yama, his other son, is lord of the Bharaṇī nakṣatra; the Aśvins preside over Aśvinī.

Read astronomically

A light too strong to look at, which must be “trimmed” before it can be lived with — the same idea as astaṅga, where a planet beside the Sun is lost in his glare.

☽
Graha two · Chandra
☽

Soma and his twenty-seven brides

the curse that made the Moon wax and wane

Dakṣa Prajāpati gave his daughters — the nakṣatras — in marriage to Soma, the Moon. But Soma loved only one: Rohiṇī, and he lingered in her house while the others waited. The sisters complained to their father; twice Soma promised to treat them equally, and twice he returned to Rohiṇī.

Dakṣa cursed him with a wasting sickness (rāja-yakṣmā), and the Moon began to fade. As he dwindled, the plants and creatures that drew life from him faded too. At the shore of Prabhāsa he bathed and worshipped, and the curse was softened rather than lifted: he would waste for half the month and grow again for the other half. Śiva set the slender crescent in his hair — Chandraśekhara — and the shrine at Prabhāsa is remembered as Somnāth.

Vedic The oldest form is in the Taittirīya Saṁhitā 2.3.5: Prajāpati gives Soma thirty-three daughters; Soma dwells only with Rohiṇī, and is seized by yakṣma. Epic Mahābhārata, Śalya Parva (the Prabhāsa tīrtha). Later Purāṇas fix the number at twenty-seven.

Read astronomically

The Moon visits one wife a night — roughly one nakṣatra a day around a 27⅓-day circuit. He “wastes” and “recovers” every month: the phases. And Rohiṇī is truly his favourite — the Moon is exalted in Taurus, the sign that holds Rohiṇī.

Also told of Soma

Soma was born from the eye of the sage Atri — or, in another telling, rose from the churned Ocean of Milk. Ṛgveda book 9 is entirely to Soma, the sacred plant-drink the Moon later came to embody.

♂
Graha three · Maṅgala
♂

The son of the Earth

Bhauma, Kuja, Aṅgāraka

Every name of Mars says where he came from: Bhauma, “of Bhūmi”; Kuja, “born of ku, the earth”; Bhūmi-putra, the Earth's son. The texts agree on the mother and differ on the father.

In one telling, when Viṣṇu as the boar Varāha lifted the drowned Earth from the cosmic waters, Bhūdevī bore him a son — red, fiery, and armed. In another, a drop of Śiva's sweat, fallen to the ground during his austerities, was received by the Earth and became a blazing child, whom Śiva raised to the sky as a graha. His name Aṅgāraka, “the glowing coal”, keeps that fire.

In Tamil devotion Maṅgala is closely linked with Murugan, the warrior-son of Śiva, and his shrine among the Navagraha temples is Vaitheeswaran Koil, where Śiva is worshipped as the healer.

Purāṇic Varāha–Bhūdevī: Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa. Śiva's sweat: Skanda Purāṇa and later tellings. Tradition the Murugan link and the Vaitheeswaran Koil shrine.

Jyotiṣa echo

Born of the Earth, Mars is the kāraka of land and property (bhūmi); born of fire and armed, of courage, siblings, blood and surgery. The story is the kārakatva.

Read astronomically

The red planet that glows like an ember — no other graha has so plain a colour, and every one of his names points to it.

☿
Graha four · Budha
☿

The child whose father was a question

Soma, Tārā and the birth of Budha

Soma, proud after his great rājasūya sacrifice, carried off Tārā, wife of his own teacher Bṛhaspati. The devas took Bṛhaspati's side, the asuras — led by Śukra — took Soma's, and the war that followed, the Tārakāmaya, shook the worlds until Brahmā made Soma give her back.

Tārā returned with child. When the boy was born he was so radiant that both Soma and Bṛhaspati claimed him. Tārā, ashamed, would not say; the child himself demanded to know his father. Pressed by Brahmā, she named Soma. The child was called Budha, “the wise one”, and his son by Ilā, Purūravas, began the lunar dynasty (Chandra-vaṁśa) of the Mahābhārata kings.

Purāṇic Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.6; Bhāgavata Purāṇa 9.14.

Jyotiṣa echo

This is the lore behind a puzzle in the friendship tables: Jupiter treats Mercury as an enemy, but Mercury does not return it, and Mercury is hostile to the Moon. The Dhaṉusu chapter of the Twelve Lagnas uses exactly this story to explain the 7th lord's awkwardness for a Sagittarius lagna.

Read astronomically

Mercury never strays far from the Sun and changes its nature with whatever it joins — a planet of mixed parentage, as its story says.

♃
Graha five · Bṛhaspati
♃

The priest who sang the cows free

Bṛhaspati, guru of the devas

Bṛhaspati — also Brahmaṇaspati, “lord of the sacred word” — is a son of the sage Aṅgiras and the priest and teacher of the gods. In the Ṛgveda he is the power of prayer itself.

The demon Vala had shut the cows of light inside a mountain. Bṛhaspati did not break the rock by force: with his band of singers he split it with a roar of sacred chant, and the cows — the dawns, the light — came pouring out. The hymn compares him to thunder bursting a cloud.

As guru of the devas he is the rival of Śukra, guru of the asuras; and as husband of Tārā he stands at the centre of Budha's story.

Vedic Ṛgveda 10.68 (Bṛhaspati and Vala); hymns to Bṛhaspati in books 2 and 4. Purāṇic parentage and the rivalry with Śukra, Mahābhārata and Purāṇas.

Jyotiṣa echo

Guru is the kāraka of wisdom, dharma, mantra and the teacher; he rules Puṣya as its deity — the nakṣatra held most auspicious for beginnings — and his aspect “releases” whatever it touches, as his chant released the light.

Two gurus, two camps

Bṛhaspati's friends (Sun, Moon, Mars) and Śukra's (Mercury, Saturn) form the two graha camps of the friendship tables — the deva party and the asura party.

♀
Graha six · Śukra
♀

The guru who could raise the dead

Śukra, Kaca and Devayānī

Śukra — Uśanas Kāvya, son of Bhṛgu — was guru of the asuras and alone knew the mṛta-sañjīvanī, the lore that restores the slain to life. However many asuras fell in battle, he raised them again.

The devas sent Kaca, Bṛhaspati's son, to become Śukra's disciple. Śukra's daughter Devayānī loved him. The suspicious asuras killed Kaca again and again; each time Devayānī begged and Śukra revived him. At last they burned him, mixed his ashes in wine, and gave it to Śukra to drink. To save Kaca now, Śukra had to teach him the sañjīvanī inside his own belly, so that Kaca could burst out and revive his teacher in turn. Kaca left with the knowledge — refusing Devayānī's hand, since one born from the guru's body is the guru's child — and Śukra forbade wine to brahmins ever after.

In a popular retelling of the Vāmana story, Śukra warned King Bali not to grant the dwarf his three steps, then shrank into the spout of the water-vessel to stop the gift — and lost an eye to the blade of grass Vāmana used to clear it.

Epic Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva (Kaca and Devayānī). Purāṇic Śukra's warning to Bali, Bhāgavata Purāṇa book 8. Retelling the lost eye. Vedic Uśanā Kāvya appears in the Ṛgveda as a seer-poet.

Jyotiṣa echo

Śukra is kalatra-kāraka — love, desire and marriage — and here he is a story of love, wine and devotion. His power over life returning is why Venus is also tied to vitality and the reproductive fluid (śukra).

Read astronomically

The brightest planet, the morning and evening star — the one light that “comes back” on the other side of the Sun every few months.

♄
Graha seven · Śani
♄

The gaze that must be lowered

Śani, the slow one

Śani is the son of Sūrya and Chāyā, the shadow — dark where his father is bright. His name Śanaiścara means “the one who moves slowly” (śanaiḥ carati).

Lost in devotion, Śani once ignored his own wife when she came to him; stung, she cursed him that whatever he looked upon would be ruined. From then on he kept his eyes lowered. When Pārvatī's son was born, the gods came to see the child, and Śani — at Pārvatī's own insistence — raised his eyes for a moment. The infant's head was struck off; Viṣṇu brought back an elephant's head, and the child became Gaṇeśa.

In the South, Śani's great shrine is Thirunallar, where King Nala is said to have been freed of the affliction that had cost him his kingdom — the story of Śani's long, testing hold that every Śani-daśā and sāḍe-sāti recalls.

Purāṇic Brahmavaivarta Purāṇa, Gaṇapati Khaṇḍa. Tradition Thirunallar and Nala.

Jyotiṣa echo

Śani's dṛṣṭi is feared above all — his special 3rd and 10th aspects, his slow transits, the seven-and-a-half years of sāḍe-sāti. The story is a lesson in where his gaze falls.

Read astronomically

The slowest of the visible planets — about 29½ years to circle the zodiac, some 2½ years in each sign. “The slow mover” is simply what Saturn is.

☊
Grahas eight & nine · Rāhu and Ketu
☊

The thief at the churning

Svarbhānu, who became the eclipse

When the devas and asuras churned the Ocean of Milk with Mount Mandara and the serpent Vāsuki, the last treasure to rise was amṛta, the nectar of immortality. Viṣṇu, as the enchantress Mohinī, served it to the devas alone.

The asura Svarbhānu, son of Siṁhikā, slipped in disguise between the Sun and the Moon and drank. They cried out; Viṣṇu's discus took off his head before the nectar could pass his throat. But the head had tasted immortality and could not die. It lives on as Rāhu, the body as Ketu — and Rāhu still pursues the two lights that betrayed him, swallowing them at the eclipse, only for them to slip out through his severed throat.

Vedic Ṛgveda 5.40.5–9: Svarbhānu pierces the Sun with darkness, and the sage Atri restores it. Purāṇic & epic Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva (the churning); Viṣṇu Purāṇa 1.9; Bhāgavata Purāṇa 8.9.

Read astronomically

Two halves, always 180° apart, immortal, that seize the Sun and Moon — the exact description of the lunar nodes, the two points where every eclipse happens. See The Sky Behind the Symbols, Part 4.

The oldest eclipse record

The Ṛgveda's Svarbhānu has no Rāhu or Ketu yet — just a darkness that seizes the Sun. The severed head and body are the Purāṇic layer, which splits the one shadow into the two nodes Jyotiṣa needs.

Mṛgaśīrṣa · Ārdrā · Rohiṇī
➶

The stag, the archer and the arrow

a hunt written across Orion

The Aitareya Brāhmaṇa tells that Prajāpati took the form of a stag (mṛga) to pursue his daughter, who had become a hind — Rohiṇī. The gods, appalled, gathered their most terrible forms into one being, Rudra, and sent him to stop it. Rudra loosed an arrow and pierced the stag.

All three were set in the sky. The stag became Mṛgaśīrṣa, “the deer's head”; the three-jointed arrow still flies in a straight line beside it; the hunter is Mṛgavyādha; and Rohiṇī shines nearby in Taurus. The next mansion, Ārdrā — “the moist one”, the tear — belongs to Rudra himself.

Vedic Aitareya Brāhmaṇa 3.33. Deities of Mṛgaśīrṣa (Soma) and Ārdrā (Rudra) per Taittirīya Saṁhitā 4.4.10.

Read astronomically

Look at Orion: λ Orionis is Mṛgaśīrṣa's star, the belt is the three-part arrow, Sirius (Mṛgavyādha) is the hunter behind it, and Betelgeuse — Ārdrā — glows red on Orion's shoulder. Aldebaran, Rohiṇī, stands just west.

Living tradition

Ārdrā is Śiva's own star: Ārudrā Darśanam on Mārgaḻi Thiruvathirai celebrates Naṭarāja's cosmic dance at Chidambaram.

Kṛttikā · the Pleiades
✲

Six mothers for a son of fire

the Kṛttikās and Kārttikeya

The Kṛttikās were the wives of the Seven Sages (the Saptarṣi of the Great Bear). Agni desired them; Svāhā, who loved Agni, took the forms of six of them — all but the faithful Arundhatī — and from her union with Agni the radiant child Skanda was born. The sages, believing their wives untrue, put six of them away; they rose to the sky as the Pleiades, while Arundhatī stayed at her husband's side.

The six Kṛttikās took the infant and nursed him together; he grew six faces to drink from all of them, and was named Kārttikeya, “son of the Kṛttikās” — Murugan, Ṣaṇmukha, the six-faced.

Epic Mahābhārata, Vana Parva (the birth of Skanda). Vedic Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa 2.1.2: the Kṛttikās were once wives of the Bears (ṛkṣas) and were kept apart from them; they “do not swerve from the east.”

Read astronomically

Why does the Vedic list begin with Kṛttikā, not Aśvinī? When the Brāhmaṇa says they rise due east, the Pleiades sat near the spring equinox — the star-calendar's natural starting point thousands of years ago. Precession has since slid the start back to Aśvinī. See Ayanāṁśa.

Living tradition

Kārttikai Dīpam — the great flame on the hill at Tiruvannamalai — is lit when the full Moon meets Kṛttikā.

Abhijit · the twenty-eighth
✧

The star that fell from the sky

how twenty-eight became twenty-seven

In the Mahābhārata, Indra comes troubled to young Skanda: Abhijit, the younger sister of Rohiṇī, jealous of her elder's place, has gone to the forest to perform austerities — and has fallen from the sky. A place in the heavens stands empty, and the reckoning of time is thrown out.

Skanda counsels that time be counted afresh; Kṛttikā rises to the sky to fill the gap, and the order of the stars is restored. The Vedic hymns had counted twenty-eight mansions with Abhijit; the zodiac of Jyotiṣa keeps twenty-seven.

Epic Mahābhārata, Vana Parva (Mārkaṇḍeya's narration to the Pāṇḍavas). Vedic Atharvaveda 19.7 hymns twenty-eight nakṣatras, Abhijit among them.

Jyotiṣa echo

Abhijit survives in muhūrta: 6°40′–10°53′20″ Capricorn, the last pada of Uttarāṣāḍhā and the first fifteenth of Śravaṇa — and the midday Abhijit muhūrta, held to conquer obstacles (abhi-jit, “victorious”).

Read astronomically

A story of a calendar being re-anchored — the memory of astronomers resetting where the count of mansions begins.

Living tradition · the Tamil year

Festivals named for the Moon and a star

Many Tamil festivals are simply a month + a nakṣatra: the day the Moon reaches that star in that solar month — usually close to the full Moon.

Thai Pūsam

Puṣya in Thai — Murugan receives the Vel; kāvaḍi at Palani.

Māsi Magam

Maghā in Māsi — sacred bathing; every twelve years, with Jupiter in Leo, the Mahāmaham at Kumbakonam.

Panguni Uttiram

Uttara Phalgunī in Panguni — the day of divine weddings: Śiva–Pārvatī, Murugan–Deivānai.

Vaikāsi Visākam

Viśākhā in Vaikāsi — Murugan's birth star.

Āḍi Pūram

Pūrva Phalgunī in Āḍi — Āṇḍāl's birth; the Goddess's festival.

Āvaṇi Tiruvōṇam

Śravaṇa — Onam, the return of King Mahābali, whom Vāmana humbled with three strides.

Kārttikai Dīpam

Kṛttikā in Kārttikai — the flame on Arunachala.

Mārgaḻi Thiruvathirai

Ārdrā in Mārgaḻi — Ārudrā Darśanam of Naṭarāja.

Mahāmaham is a festival set by a graha as well as a star: it needs Jupiter, which takes about twelve years to come back to Leo.
